1d:Te02,<h2>Context</h2>
<p>The product was live, multi-tenant and queue-driven on Kubernetes, with customer integrations that wrote into their own buckets. It needed to move into new AWS accounts. A program team ran the schedule; my part was the infrastructure.</p>
<h2>Problem</h2>
<ul>
<li>Nothing could be lifted and shifted: the old production account had years of drift, 113 IAM users and 99 long-lived access keys.</li>
<li>Account-level changes are hard to undo, and they silently break anything that depends on account or organization conditions in resource policies.</li>
<li>Tens of terabytes of customer data had to move with proof that nothing was lost, while the old account kept serving traffic.</li>
</ul>
<h2>What I did</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Built the new accounts from live specs, not stale code.</strong> I wrote the Terraform for the new QA, production, shared-services and sandbox accounts (3 EKS clusters with Helm, IRSA and KEDA, Amazon MQ, Redis, CloudFront with WAF, Lambda, ECS, VPN) from resource specs read through the cloud APIs. That exposed drift the old code hid, such as a Redis cluster that had quietly moved on four settings. QA ran end to end in about five weeks.</li>
<li><strong>Rehearsed the risky changes on a disposable account.</strong> I built a canary account that mirrors every managed service in use, wired it with a 13-probe validation suite, and ran the account-level changes on it first. EKS, IRSA, load balancers and KEDA came through intact, and the probes caught a stale admin trust and a policy that blocked CloudTrail changes before production was anywhere near them.</li>
<li><strong>Proved the new IAM was narrower, not just different.</strong> I replayed every successful API action from 18.5 months of CloudTrail (about 1.3 TB) through roughly 1,100 IAM policy simulations, using the exact resource strings the applications use. The new roles shipped tighter than the old ones, and every class of access error after cutover was fixed.</li>
<li><strong>Moved the data with verification, not hope.</strong> 36+ TB and 125M+ objects moved across accounts at 99.997% verified fidelity, with two-way replication and per-tenant KMS keys for customer integration buckets, and per-bucket cost and status reporting. When a set of buckets was declared "already migrated", diffing object keys showed zero overlap, so they were not deleted until the owners signed off.</li>
<li><strong>Made CI deploy to both worlds at once.</strong> One pipeline deployed to the old account and the new ones side by side, with a strict per-account OIDC role resolver and a guard that fails the job if it lands in the wrong account. That let tenants move one at a time.</li>
<li><strong>Hardened CI trust on the way.</strong> Personal access tokens were replaced with 7 least-privilege GitHub Apps, and 40 self-hosted runners were registered with OIDC trust per account.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Impact</h2>
<ul>
<li>Old production capacity was cut by 60% once traffic had moved, with zero 5xx errors across the sampled requests.</li>
<li>The new production account started with a measured security baseline: IAM users 113 to 1, static access keys 99 to 0, roles 371 to 72, internet-open security groups 36 to 4, and GuardDuty findings surfacing in 15 minutes instead of 6 hours.</li>
</ul>
<h2>What I would do differently</h2>
<p>Start the CloudTrail replay on day one rather than after the first roles existed. It was the single most convincing piece of evidence in every access review, and having it earlier would have shaped the role design instead of validating it.</p>
